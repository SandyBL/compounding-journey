When I first started investing, I assumed a fund named "Global Growth" or "Conservative Income" simply reflected the manager's general vibe or marketing pitch. I thought the portfolio manager had complete freedom to buy whatever looked good that week.

It wasn't until I read the actual rules that I realized fund names are strict, legally binding contracts. In the United States the **SEC's Names Rule** does this work; in the United Kingdom the **FCA** polices fund names through its fair, clear and not misleading standard and, for anything claiming to be sustainable, through the Sustainability Disclosure Requirements naming and marketing rules. Both regulators enforce mathematical boundaries on what a fund may call itself, precisely to stop the marketing department from writing the investment policy.

Understanding what these names and categories actually mean under the hood is one of the most effective ways to ensure your money is taking on the exact level of risk you intended. Let's break down the technical metrics, asset thresholds, and category definitions that govern your investment funds.

> 💡 **The Core Foundation:**
> A fund's commercial name isn't advertising—it's a legal boundary. Under the SEC's Names Rule, a fund whose name suggests a particular type of investment must adopt a policy of putting at least **80% of its assets** there, and must check compliance quarterly.

### The 80% Rule, and What the 2023 Amendments Changed

Rule 35d-1 has required an 80% investment policy since 2001, but the SEC's 2023 amendments widened it considerably. The rule now reaches names suggesting a **strategy or characteristic**—"growth," "value," "sustainable," "ESG"—not merely an asset class or geography. Funds must document how they define the terms in their own name, test the 80% basket quarterly, and get back into compliance promptly after a drift. "Temporary defensive position" is a permitted reason to breach it; a change of mind is not.

The FCA arrived at the same place from a different direction. Its **SDR labelling regime** restricts terms such as "sustainable," "impact" and "transition" to funds that qualify for a specific label and hold at least 70% of assets in line with the stated objective, and its anti-greenwashing rule applies to every regulated firm's communications regardless of labels. The practical upshot for a UK investor is that a fund renamed in 2024 or 2025 usually renamed for a regulatory reason, and reading the prospectus tells you which one.

## Money Market Funds: The Metrics of Capital Preservation

Money market funds are designed for extreme safety, short-term liquidity, and capital preservation. They invest exclusively in high-quality, short-term debt instruments and bank deposits. To ensure these funds don't quietly take on hidden interest rate or default risks, regulators enforce two critical technical metrics:

* **Weighted Average Maturity (WAM):** Measures the portfolio's sensitivity to interest rate shifts. US money market funds under **SEC Rule 2a-7** cap WAM at **60 days**; UK and European short-term MMFs apply the same 60-day ceiling, while standard MMFs may run to 6 months.
* **Weighted Average Life (WAL):** Measures the portfolio's credit and default risk by tracking the time remaining until the final principal payoff of every asset. Rule 2a-7 caps WAL at **120 days** for US funds, matched by the short-term MMF limit in the UK; standard MMFs allow up to 12 months.

Rule 2a-7 adds two features worth knowing if you park cash in a US money fund: minimum daily and weekly liquid asset requirements, and—following the 2023 reforms—**mandatory liquidity fees** on institutional prime and tax-exempt funds during heavy redemption days, replacing the redemption gates that were used in 2020. Government money market funds, which is what most retail cash sits in, remain at a stable $1.00 share price.

[ WAM ≤ 60 Days ]  ➔ Interest Rate Sensitivity Cap
[ WAL ≤ 120 Days ] ➔ Credit & Principal Default Risk Cap

Money market funds are strictly prohibited from holding direct exposure to equities or unhedged foreign currencies, ensuring that your cash reserve remains liquid and stable.

> 💡 **A Simple Analogy:**
> Think of WAM as how quickly a sailboat can adjust its sails when the wind (interest rates) shifts tomorrow. Think of WAL as the total distance on the map until the boat reaches a safe harbor (when the borrower pays back your principal).

## Quantitative Asset Thresholds: Fixed Income vs. Equities

For standard, non-money market funds, regulatory guidelines establish clear mathematical thresholds regarding how much capital must be allocated to stocks versus bonds:

In the UK, these bands are not vague marketing tiers but the **Investment Association (IA) sectors** every fund is sorted into, and they are the most useful thing on a factsheet:

| IA Sector (UK) | Equity Exposure Range | Fixed Income Role | Risk Profile |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **UK Gilts / Corporate Bond** | **0% Equities** | 100% Bonds / Cash | Low risk, income focus |
| **Mixed Investment 0–35% Shares** | **Up to 35% Equities** | Dominant bond allocation | Moderate risk, slight growth |
| **Mixed Investment 20–60% Shares** | **20% to 60% Equities** | Balanced bond allocation | Balanced growth and income |
| **Mixed Investment 40–85% Shares** | **40% to 85% Equities** | Reduced bond allocation | Growth-tilted |
| **Global / UK All Companies** | **80%+ Equities** | Residual cash only | High growth, higher volatility |

US funds have no equivalent statutory band system. Instead, the 80% Names Rule policy does the work—an "equity income" fund must hold 80% in equities—and Morningstar's **target-allocation categories** (Conservative, Moderate, Aggressive, at 15–30%, 50–70% and 70–85% equity respectively) provide the comparison shorthand that the IA sectors provide in Britain. Neither is a guarantee: a US "balanced" fund can sit anywhere its prospectus permits, so the prospectus is the document that binds it.

### Currency and Geographic Mandates
Beyond the stock-versus-bond split, geographic labels carry real weight. Under the SEC's Names Rule, a fund called "US Large Cap" or "European Equity" must keep 80% of assets in investments matching that description. The genuinely useful question for a UK investor is different: whether the fund **hedges the currency back to sterling**. A global equity fund bought unhedged carries dollar exposure whether or not you wanted it, and a hedged share class—usually flagged "GBP Hedged" in the name—removes it at a cost. US investors buying international funds face the mirror image of the same decision.

## Specialized Categories: Indexing, Guaranteed, and Absolute Return

Regulators also define clear frameworks for structured and non-traditional investment strategies:

### 1. Passive & Index Funds
These funds (including Index Funds and ETFs) are designed to replicate or track a specific market index. Because they mirror a market benchmark passively, regulators grant them exemptions from standard single-stock concentration limits, allowing them to match the exact weights of large-cap indices.

### 2. Guaranteed and Defined-Outcome Funds
These products depend on an explicit guarantee from a third-party institution, usually a bank or insurer. The guaranteed *fund* is a largely European format; in the US the same engineering is sold as structured notes, market-linked CDs or buffered ETFs, and in the UK as structured deposits and structured products. The distinction that matters in both countries is whether the protection is a **deposit**—FDIC-insured in the US, FSCS-covered to £85,000 in the UK—or merely the **unsecured promise of the issuing bank**.

### 3. Absolute Return Funds
These funds aim for positive returns across all market conditions regardless of whether the broader stock market goes up or down. They use active long/short strategies and derivatives to uncouple their performance from traditional stock benchmarks.

### 4. Interval Funds and Non-Traded Vehicles (US)
Worth flagging because the name hides the most important feature. An **interval fund** is a registered closed-end fund that only offers to repurchase shares periodically—typically 5% of assets per quarter—which lets it hold far more illiquid credit and property than an open-ended fund may. It is a legitimate structure, but "fund" in the name does not imply you can get out on Tuesday.

### 5. Flexible / Global Funds
Flexible funds give managers the maximum operational freedom. They operate without fixed percentage limits on equities, bonds, or currencies, allowing the manager to adjust asset allocations rapidly as economic conditions change.
