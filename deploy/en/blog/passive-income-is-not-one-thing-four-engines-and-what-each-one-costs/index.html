Passive Income Is Not One Thing: Four Engines and What Each One Costs

A dividend fund, a rented flat, a bond ladder and a product you sold once are four different machines. They differ in the capital they need, the hours they quietly demand, and how they break.

  3. What each engine actually costs you
  4. The mistake I see most often

Passive income gets sold as a single idea, as though a dividend fund, a rented flat and an online course were the same thing in different clothes. They are not. They are four distinct machines with different fuel, different maintenance and different ways of failing.

Confusing them is what produces the two classic mistakes: buying the highest yield available and calling it income, and buying something described as passive that turns out to be a second job.

The one number that makes them comparable

Every income engine can be reduced to the same question: how much capital does €1,000 a month require?

€1,000 a month is €12,000 a year. Divide that by the net yield and you have the capital.

EngineRealistic net yieldCapital for €1,000/monthHours it really takes
Dividends from listed shares or funds2–4%€300,000–€600,000almost none
Interest: deposits, bonds, bond funds2–4%€300,000–€600,000almost none
Rental property3–5% net of costs€240,000–€400,000 plus purchase costsreal and recurring
Products, royalties, a small businessno fixed relationshiplittle capital, much timehigh at the start, never zero

Two things fall out of that table immediately.

The first is that the financial engines need serious capital and offer nothing else. There is no yield that turns €20,000 into a living. Anything advertising one is selling you risk, a return of your own capital, or a fiction.

The second is that the fourth row is a different species. It substitutes time for capital. That makes it the only route open to someone who has no capital yet, and it is also the one that most resembles work.

The distinction that matters: The first three engines convert capital into income. The fourth converts effort into an asset that later produces income. If you have capital, you are choosing an allocation. If you do not, you are choosing a project — and the project has to be finished before it pays anything.

Yield is not return, and chasing it is expensive

The most common expensive mistake in this whole area is treating yield as the score.

Total return is capital growth plus income. Yield is only the income part. A fund yielding 7% while its capital value falls 5% a year has handed you 2%, and it has handed it to you in the least tax-efficient shape.

High yields are usually high for a reason: a business paying out more than it earns, a property in a place people are leaving, a bond whose issuer might not repay. Yield is often the market's estimate of risk, expressed as a number people mistake for a reward.

The unglamorous version works better. Own productive assets for total return while you are building. Convert to income when you actually need income — which for most people means selling a small, planned amount rather than engineering a portfolio that pays exactly the right dividend.

What each engine actually costs you

Dividends and interest. The genuinely passive ones. You will be taxed as you receive them, in most places whether you needed the money that year or not, which is why they are a mediocre way to hold money you were going to reinvest anyway. Their weakness is that the yield is what it is; there is no lever.

Rental property. Higher headline yields, and the gap between gross and net eats most of the difference: vacancy, repairs, agency fees, insurance, taxes, the bad tenant that arrives eventually. Concentrated — one asset, one street, one legal system — illiquid, and demanding of your attention in exactly the months you had other plans. Leverage magnifies both the returns and the mistakes.

Products and royalties. No capital barrier, no floor either. Income is volatile and often decays: a course goes out of date, a platform changes its rules, the traffic dries up. What looks like passive income here is usually the tail of continuous work. Sometimes an excellent tail.

The mistake I see most often

People build the engine before the fuel. They spend a year building something to generate €200 a month while saving nothing, and end that year with €2,400 of income and no assets.

The boring order is nearly always better: build capital first with the highest savings rate you can hold, keep it invested for total return, and let income engines be something you turn on when you have the capital to make them meaningful. A 4% yield is a rounding error on €10,000 and a salary on €400,000. The interesting variable is the capital, not the yield.

The exception is real: if your engine is a product or a skill, building it early can raise your income, which raises your savings rate, which builds capital faster. That is a good reason to build one. "Because it is passive" is not.

Play it forward before you commit

The Passive Income Engine exists to make that comparison concrete. You build a portfolio of income-producing assets, advance through time and watch what the cash flow does — how long each engine takes to cover a real bill, what happens to the timeline when you add capital instead of adding another project, and what a 2% difference in net yield does over twenty years.

Run it twice. Once the way people describe passive income, with several small engines started early. Once the boring way, with capital compounding first and income switched on later. The comparison is usually the most useful hour you will spend on this topic — and it costs nothing except the hour.
